How to use Part II

Ten jobs, each written as the job rather than as a tab, each stating who it is for before it starts.

Every case below opens with two lines - For and Assumes - so you can tell in five seconds whether it is yours. If a case assumes Part I, it assumes only Part I: that you can open a file, read back what the app says about it, and convert it.

The pictures in these cases were taken by driving the built application over a synthetic survey created for the purpose. No real survey, filename, site or coordinate appears in any of them, so what you see on your own data will differ in the numbers and not in the layout.

1. First morning on a crew: identify an unknown file and report it

For: a first-day observer, or anyone handed a file with no covering note.
Assumes: nothing beyond Part I.

The job. Somebody hands you a memory stick with a file on it and asks "what is this?". You need an answer you can put in an email in ten minutes.

  1. Open it and let the app identify it. Press 1, click Pick a seismic file… and choose the file. You are not asked what format it is: SeisConv reads the file and determines the format, the revision, the sample encoding and the byte order for itself. The panel then states what it found - see .
  2. Write down the five facts it gives you. Format, revision, trace count, samples per trace, sample interval. From the last two you get the record length: samples × interval. 1000 samples at 2 ms is a 2-second record.
  3. Look at one trace (2). Step a few traces with Next ›. The header panel beside the plot shows that trace's own header fields - the shot identifier, the channel, and the coordinates if the recorder wrote any. If the coordinate fields are all zero, this file has no geometry, which is a fact worth reporting on its own.
  4. Look at the whole record (3), with AGC on. You are answering one question: does this look like a shot record - a first-arrival pattern fanning out from a near-trace - or does it look like noise, a monitor sweep, or a test?
  5. Report it. A defensible report is one paragraph: the format and revision, the trace and sample counts, the sample interval and record length, whether trace headers carry coordinates, and what the section looks like. Say nothing you did not read off the app.

If the file will not open at all, do not guess - go to recipe 10, which is written for exactly that.

2. SEG-D in, SEG-Y Rev 1 out: one file, then a whole folder

For: an observer or processor turning a day's field records into what processing asked for.
Assumes: Part I, and that you know which revision the receiving system wants. If nobody said, ask; if nobody answers, Rev 1 is the safe assumption.

Do one file first. Never batch a folder you have not proven with a single file - a batch multiplies a mistake by the number of shots.

  1. Open one SEG-D record on the Converter and read back what SeisConv found.
  2. Click the SEG-Y Rev 1 output chip.
  3. In the name builder, switch on Original name and Format so the output stays traceable to its input, as in .
  4. Convert & Save…, choose the destination, confirm.
  5. Prove it. Open the SEG-Y you just wrote. Trace count, sample count and sample interval must equal the SEG-D's. Press 3 and check the section is the same record. If any of the three numbers changed, stop and find out why before you convert 400 more.

Then the folder.

  1. Press Ctrl+B. The Converter switches to Convert a folder (batch).
  2. Pick folder… - the folder of SEG-D records. The panel lists what it found, and the run button names the count ("Convert 3 files"), as in .
  3. Choose SEG-Y Rev 1 again - the format is chosen per mode, so set it here too.
  4. Pick destination… - a different folder from the inputs. Writing outputs beside inputs is how a folder becomes unsortable.
  5. Click the run button. Progress is reported per file; Cancel stops a run in progress, and Open folder appears at the end.

Check the batch, not just the last file. Count the outputs against the inputs - if the numbers differ, the run reported errors and you need to read them. Then open the first, one middle, and the last output and compare their trace and sample counts with their inputs. Three spot checks catch a systematic fault; zero checks catch nothing.

Two batch behaviours worth knowing. Sequence # in the name builder exists only in folder mode, and numbers the outputs in the order they are processed. And Tape Image is offered in folder mode only, because it combines a whole folder into one archive container rather than converting file-for-file.

3. A shot looks wrong: geophone, ground or recorder?

For: an observer with a record that does not look like the others.
Assumes: Part I, and that you can open the suspect record and a known-good one from the same line.

The job. One record looks bad. Before anyone re-shoots it, you want to say which of three things it is: a channel fault (one geophone or its cable), a ground condition (wind, traffic, a soft spot - real noise, arriving at real sensors), or an instrument fault (something the recorder did to every channel at once). The three leave different fingerprints, and the three tools below separate them.

Step 1 - let the health scan sort the traces for you

Open the record, press 3 for the File Viewer, and click Health scan. Every trace is judged against its local neighbours rather than against a fixed threshold, which is what makes the result meaningful on a record whose amplitude falls off with offset. The detectors flag traces that are dead, noisy, hot or weak in amplitude, clipped or spiky, or reversed in polarity.

Read the pattern, not just the count:

  • One or two isolated flagged traces, their neighbours clean - a channel fault. Geophone, take-out, or that pair of cable conductors. Check the plant.
  • A contiguous run of flagged traces - something physical at that part of the spread: a road, a stream, a stretch of bad plant, one bad cable section.
  • Every trace flagged, or nearly every one - suspect the recorder or the source, not the spread. One geophone cannot make the whole record bad.
  • Nothing flagged, but the record still looks wrong - the fault is not amplitude, so go to the spectrum below.

Sensitivity… tunes each detector between Low (stricter, fewer flags) and High, and the flagged count updates live without re-scanning - useful when a scan flags half the record and you want to see which traces are worst rather than which are merely unusual. Export report… writes the flagged traces to CSV with the per-detector score, confidence, reason and the local baseline it was judged against, which is what you attach to a re-shoot request. Clear flags removes the overlay again.

Step 2 - ask what frequencies are in it

Press 8 for the Spectrum tab and read the average amplitude spectrum over a range of traces (From / To, then Recompute).

  • A sharp spike at the mains frequency, and its harmonics - electrical pick-up, from a powerline or a generator. That is the environment, not the geophone.
  • Energy heaped at the very low end with nothing above it - wind or swell on the phones, or ground roll dominating everything.
  • A spectrum that simply stops at some frequency - a filter is in circuit, in the recorder. Check the instrument settings.
  • Flat, featureless, no roll-off at all - that is what a dead or disconnected channel looks like in the frequency domain.

The F-K display separates coherent noise from signal in a way the average spectrum cannot: it plots energy against frequency and wavenumber, so slow, steeply-dipping ground roll spreads out along the wavenumber axis while flat-lying reflections stay near the centre. If your record is dominated by a strong low-velocity fan, it is ground roll, and no amount of re-shooting the same spread will remove it.

Step 3 - compare the suspect trace against a good one

Two traces side by side settle most arguments. On the Trace Inspector, step to the suspect trace and click Add to Workbench; step to a clean neighbour and add that too. Press 9 for the Trace Workbench, where the collected traces can be drawn Overlay or Side-by-side.

With two traces collected, the Workbench also offers an A / B selection, a difference trace and a cross-correlation. The correlation peak tells you whether the two traces carry the same event at a shifted time (a timing problem) or nothing in common at all (a channel problem). Invert flips a trace's polarity on screen - if inverting makes the suspect trace match its neighbour, you have found a reversed geophone, and the invert is display-only so nothing in the file changes.

Step 4 - first breaks, when you need timing evidence

Back on the File Viewer, First breaks opens the assisted onset picker. Drop at least two seed picks on traces you trust, then Assisted fill picks the rest along the moveout you implied, inside the ±ms window. An auto-pick is a first guess to edit, not an answer: Reject flagged drops the low-confidence and off-trend picks, Accept all confirms what remains, Clear picks starts over, and Export CSV… writes them out. A first-break curve that is smooth except for one trace sitting off the trend is timing evidence for that channel; a whole record whose first breaks are shifted by a constant is evidence about the source or the trigger, not the spread.

Step 5 - is there any signal in it at all?

If the argument is whether the record is usable rather than which channel is at fault, press 7 for the Velocity tab and Compute semblance. Semblance measures how coherently events line up across the spread when moveout is removed at each trial velocity, so a record with real reflections shows bright, isolated peaks; a record of noise shows a smeared panel with nothing to pick.

The verdict you can defend names the evidence: which traces the health scan flagged and against what local baseline, what the spectrum showed, and whether a comparison trace from the same spread behaves differently. "Channel 47 is dead" is a claim; "channel 47 flagged dead by the health scan, flat spectrum, no correlation with channel 46, neighbours clean" is a finding.

4. Build 2D line geometry from scratch and QC the SPS triplet

For: a surveyor or party chief laying out a new 2D line.
Assumes: Part I, and that you know the receiver interval, the source interval and the numbering the client expects. Internet is needed only for the map background; the coordinate registry and every calculation are built in and work offline.

The job. There is no pre-plot. You know roughly where the line runs and what the station intervals are, and processing needs an SPS triplet - the S, R and X files.

Step 1 - draw the line

  1. Press 5 for SPS Creation. Leave the mode on 2D: in 2D each line you draw is walked once, with sources and receivers laid along that same line.
  2. Set Add on click, then click the map at the start and end of the line. You are placing the line's vertices, not its stations - the stations are generated later at the interval you give. A straight line needs two clicks; a dog-leg needs one click per bend.
  3. For a second line, click + Next line first (a line needs at least two vertices before it can be finished), then keep clicking.
  4. Fix mistakes with Undo (Ctrl+Z on this tab), by switching to Drag points and moving a vertex, or by editing the Points table directly. Clear removes every line.

The layer ticks - Basemap, Connection lines, Direction arrows, Distance labels, Stations & numbers - each have their own opacity slider, so you can turn the map down to read the stations or the stations down to read the map. Zoom speed slows the wheel down for fine positioning.

Step 2 - confirm the coordinate system

The CRS button carries the system the app suggested from your first pick. Open it and confirm it is the one the client works in. This is the single most expensive thing to get wrong on this tab: every coordinate in the files you are about to generate is expressed in it, and a survey delivered in the wrong CRS is a survey in the wrong place.

Step 3 - clear the checks

Open the Checks pane. Findings come in three severities and only error blocks generation - warnings and information are shown for you to judge. The errors that block are: a duplicate line name, a line with fewer than two points, a zero-length segment (two consecutive stations in the same place), a duplicate station number on one line, and a preplot line with missing station numbers. Click a finding to locate it on the map, fix it, and check again.

Step 4 - generate the survey

Generate… opens the wizard. It asks for what a drawn line cannot know:

  • Receiver interval (m) and Source interval (m) - the station spacings.
  • Receiver line start / inc and Receiver point start / inc, and the same pair for sources - the numbering. SPS numbers points per line, so these decide what the processor will see when they open the files.
  • Receiver type and Source type - the code written into the SPS records.
  • Relation - Full line means every shot records into every receiver on the line; Split-spread records into the Channels (split) nearest each shot. This choice is what fills the X file, so it decides the fold of the survey.
  • Output base name - the stem the S, R and X files share.

Create & Save… writes the triplet and opens the new survey on the SPS tab. A matching ESRI .prj is written alongside when the CRS can be described, so the result opens georeferenced in a GIS.

Step 5 - QC what you generated, before it goes to the field

On the SPS tab (4), the survey is drawn with Sources, Receivers, X-ref spider, Fold/coverage and Bin grid as separate layer ticks.

  1. Look at it. Sources and receivers should sit on the line you drew, at the spacing you asked for, with no strays. A single point in the wrong place is obvious on the map and invisible in a text file.
  2. Run QC. Fill in Src int, Rcv int, Max off and Tol with what you intended, then Run QC. The report tells you where the generated survey departs from the intervals you specified. QC report exports it.
  3. Turn on the X-ref spider. Every source is drawn to the receivers that record it, so the relation you chose becomes a picture. A full-line relation fans to the whole line; a split-spread fans to a window that walks with the shot. If the spider does not look like the relation you asked for, you picked the wrong one.

Once the crew has shot and you have the field records, the same geometry can be cross-checked against what the recorder actually wrote into the trace headers. Open the seismic file and the survey together on the Geometry QC tab (6), set the station-match Tolerance in metres, and click Run geometry check. It reports how many trace headers match a station within tolerance and lists the ones that do not; clicking a finding rings that station on the SPS map. A file whose receiver coordinates match no station at all, as in the figure, is either the wrong survey for that file or a file whose headers were never stamped.

If the job is 3D

Switch the mode to 3D before generating and your picked lines change meaning: they become the receiver lines, and source lines are generated perpendicular to them. The wizard then gains Source-line spacing (m), Azimuth (° CW from N) (leave it empty to take the bearing from the longest picked line) and, for the Moving patch relation, Patch lines - how many receiver lines each shot records into. The relation names change with the mode: Full line and Split-spread become Full template and Moving patch.

3D generation lays a regular orthogonal grid. It is not a template designer, and the generated point count is capped - a very large grid is refused with a message rather than half-written. Always check the result on the SPS tab before it goes to the field.

5. A pre-plot CSV in the wrong CRS: import it, fix the mapping, check, generate

For: anyone handed someone else's coordinates.
Assumes: Part I, and that you can find out - from the sender, not by guessing - which coordinate reference system the numbers are in.

The job. A pre-plot arrives as a CSV. The columns are in an order nobody agreed, the header row may or may not be there, and the covering email says "UTM" without saying which zone. You need it in SeisConv, in the right place on the earth, and then out again as SPS.

Before anything else: establish the CRS. A coordinate pair is meaningless without the system it is expressed in. Easting 500000, northing 4000000 is a real place in every UTM zone on the planet, and a different real place in each. If you cannot get a definite answer - an EPSG code, or a zone and a hemisphere - stop and ask. Everything below is mechanical; this is the part that carries the risk.

Step 1 - read the file in

  1. Press 5 for SPS Creation, then Import plan….
  2. Choose file… for the CSV, TSV or GeoJSON - or paste the rows straight in and click Use pasted text, which is quicker when someone sent them in the body of an email.
  3. Set Delimiter (Auto-detect, Comma, Tab, Semicolon or Whitespace) and tick First row is a header if it is. The preview redraws as you change them, so you can see the columns split correctly before you map anything.

Step 2 - map the columns and declare the coordinates

  1. Map the file's columns onto line, station, the two coordinate columns and elevation. Read the preview values as you go, not just the column names: a column called "X" holding numbers around 31 is latitude, whatever it is called.
  2. Say what the two coordinate columns are: Lat / Long (WGS84) if they are degrees, or Easting / Northing if they are projected metres. Get this wrong and the plan lands in the sea.
  3. If they are eastings and northings, set the CRS below - the search box takes an EPSG code or a name, and the whole registry is built in, so it works with no internet.
  4. Swap the two coordinate columns is there for the commonest single fault in exchanged coordinate files: northing/easting written in the other order. If the preview or the resulting map puts the line somewhere impossible, try this before doubting the CRS.

Step 3 - choose what the points are, and what happens to the existing plan

  1. These points are - Receivers, Sources, or Both, co-located. There is no default; the import stays disabled until you choose.
  2. Replace the plan discards what is currently drawn; Append keeps it and adds these lines to it.
  3. Use the stations exactly as given (preplot) is the important tick. With it on, the points are treated as a pre-plot: they are used exactly where and as they are, with their own station numbers, and no layout wizard re-lays them. That is what you want when somebody else surveyed those positions. With it off, the imported points become line vertices for you to generate stations along.
  4. Import stays disabled until it has everything it needs - a file or pasted text, a column mapping, a CRS for projected input, and a point role. If it is greyed out, one of those four is missing.

Step 4 - check that it landed where it should

This is the step people skip, and it is the only one that catches a wrong CRS. Turn on the Basemap, click Fit map to plan, and look. The line should be on the ground it is supposed to be on - the right country, the right side of the road, the right wadi. A CRS mistake is not subtle at this scale: the plan lands in the wrong zone, the wrong hemisphere, or the middle of the ocean. Then read the Checks pane and clear every error, remembering that a preplot line must have a station number on every point.

Step 5 - out again as SPS

An imported pre-plot is already positioned and already numbered, so it does not need the layout wizard: the SPS 2.1 button in the plan-export row writes the S, R and X files straight out, using the stations exactly as they are. (That button is offered only when every line is a pre-plot; a line you drew still needs a station interval, so it goes through Generate… instead.) The plan can also leave as CSV, GeoJSON or KML if the recipient wants it in that shape.

When the CRS label on an existing survey is simply wrong

Sometimes what arrives is not a CSV but a finished SPS survey carrying the wrong header. Load it on the SPS tab and open Headers…, which shows the H-record block as loaded - the CRS and datum, the administrative records, and the raw records themselves.

On the CRS / Datum tab there is one choice that decides everything, and it is worth stopping to think about which one you mean:

  • Fix the label only - the coordinates are correct and the header describes them wrongly. The header is rewritten; the numbers are untouched.
  • Reproject the coordinates too - the header is right about what the coordinates were, and you want them converted into a different system. The numbers change.

Apply changes to selects whether the edit hits all the S / R / X files together or one of them, Apply commits it, and Export corrected (ZIP)… saves the edited files. Nothing is written over your originals.

6. Hand the geometry to a GIS and open it in QGIS

For: preparing a client deliverable, or getting the survey in front of someone who works in maps rather than in SPS.
Assumes: a survey loaded on the SPS tab, and that you know which CRS the recipient wants.

The job. The client wants the geometry in a GIS, and it has to arrive georeferenced - meaning the files carry enough information for the GIS to place them on the earth without anyone typing in a projection.

Pick the right export for the recipient

The export row on the SPS tab offers several, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Export shapefile (ZIP)… - sources and receivers as ESRI Shapefile layers (.shp, .shx, .dbf and a .prj), zipped together. This is the safe default for a GIS: the .prj is what tells QGIS or ArcGIS where on the earth the points are, and a shapefile without one is a set of numbers nobody can place.
  • GeoJSON and KML - single files. GeoJSON is the one to send to a web map or a script; KML is the one to send to somebody who will open it in Google Earth.
  • CSV and QC report - the point table and the QC findings, for a spreadsheet rather than a map.
  • GeoTIFF… - not points but pictures: fold, an elevation surface and the survey layout as georeferenced rasters. See below.

Choose the CRS deliberately

The Shapefile CRS box has a deliberate empty state: leave it empty and the survey's own coordinates are kept unchanged and simply described in the .prj; pick a CRS and the points are reprojected into it. Keeping the survey's own coordinates is almost always right - the points then still match the SPS files and the trace headers, and the GIS does any further conversion itself.

A survey whose CRS cannot be honestly described gets no .prj rather than a guessed one. If the .prj is missing from your ZIP, that is the app telling you the source survey never carried a CRS it could state - fix that first (recipe 5), rather than typing a projection into the GIS and hoping.

What lands where, in QGIS

  1. Unzip the export. Keep the four files together: a shapefile is not one file, and moving the .shp without its companions breaks it.
  2. Drag the .shp into QGIS. Sources and receivers arrive as separate point layers, each with its attributes - line, station, coordinates, elevation - in the attribute table.
  3. Check the layer's CRS in its properties and compare it against what you exported. If QGIS reports the CRS you expected and the points sit on the right ground under a basemap, the deliverable is correct.
  4. If the points appear in an empty part of the world, the CRS is the suspect, not the coordinates. Go back to recipe 5.

Look at it on real imagery before you send it

The SPS tab's Real map view draws the same survey over satellite imagery or street mapping. Two minutes here catches what a text file cannot show: a line crossing a river it should not, a station in a building, a receiver line running off the permit. The Map bearing control rotates the view (the N button snaps it back to north), which is how you get a line running across the page rather than diagonally.

Real map fetches tiles from the internet. The plain Survey grid view needs no network at all, and every calculation and reprojection is done locally either way.

When they want rasters: the GeoTIFF export

GeoTIFF… opens a wizard that writes georeferenced images, which is what a GIS person means by "give me the fold map". The layers are Fold / coverage (which needs the X-ref relations, so it is unavailable for a survey with no X file), Elevation surface (interpolated from the station elevations) and Survey layout (an RGB picture of the survey).

  • Whole survey + margin sets the area from every loaded station plus a margin; or Drag a box on the map… closes the dialog, lets you drag the area you want, and returns.
  • Ground resolution is metres per pixel. Use recommended sets the finest resolution the survey's station spacing actually supports - going finer than that only makes the file bigger and emptier, not more accurate.
  • Basemap optionally downloads map tiles for the area and resamples them onto the same grid. This needs internet, and the imagery stays subject to the tile provider's terms - which matters if the product is going to a client.
  • The elevation layer never extrapolates: a pixel with no station inside the search radius is written as nodata rather than an invented height. An elevation GeoTIFF with holes in it is being honest with you.

7. Vibroseis day: design a sweep, check the Klauder wavelet, compare designed against measured

For: a vibroseis crew - the observer and the vibrator mechanic.
Assumes: Part I, and that you already work with vibrators. This case is not for a first-day reader: it assumes you know what a sweep is for.

The job. The contract specifies a sweep. You have to produce it, hand the right file to the right box, and then prove that what the vibrator actually put into the ground matches what you designed.

Step 1 - design it

The Sweeps tab needs no file open. Set:

  • Type - the frequency law. Linear is a constant number of Hz per second. dB/Hz and dB/Octave dwell non-uniformly so the energy spectrum tilts by the slope you give. T-Power grows the sweep rate as time to a power, dwelling at the start frequency.
  • Start Hz and End Hz - the band. An end below the start gives a downsweep.
  • Length ms, Taper in, Taper out and the taper shape (Cosine or Blackman). The tapers keep the vibrator from slamming to full force instantly and control the ripple at the edges of the band.
  • Phase °, Amplitude, and Pilot dt - the sample interval of the exported pilot file only. It is not the vibrator's own rate, and the SCIO .SV table is always written at 2048 samples per second whatever you choose here.
  • Segmented sweep chains up to 16 segments, each with its own law, band and length, with phase kept continuous across every join.

Build sweep generates it and refreshes the four plots.

Step 2 - read the four plots

  • Pilot signal - the sweep itself, with the tapers visible at the ends.
  • Frequency vs time - the law you asked for. In a segmented sweep the joins show as slope changes, and there should be no jumps.
  • Amplitude spectrum - the in-band level and the roll-off at the edges. A non-linear sweep shows its designed tilt here, which is how you confirm you got the tilt you meant.
  • Klauder wavelet - the sweep's autocorrelation, which is the effective source wavelet you will actually have after correlation. This is the plot that tells you what the sweep buys you: a narrow main lobe means better time resolution, and the peak-to-side-lobe ratio in dB is the number to watch. A longer sweep and a wider band both sharpen it; tapers trade a little in-band energy for much cleaner spectral edges.

Step 3 - export what each part of the crew needs

  • Pilot SEG-Y… or Pilot SU… - a one-trace file of the designed pilot, for the recorder or for processing.
  • SCIO .SV… - the sweep-definition table for the sweep generator.
  • Pilot CSV… - sample number, time and amplitude, for anyone who wants the numbers.
  • Sweep sheet… - a printable HTML sheet with the parameter table, the four plots and the advisories, for the observer's book. Print it to PDF from the browser.
  • Save as… under Survey presets stores the whole design plus the QC thresholds under a name, and Export… / Import… move a preset between machines as JSON - which is how both field machines end up shooting the same specification.

A long sweep at 0.25 ms can exceed the 65,535-sample limit a SEG-Y or SU trace can carry. The exporter says so and suggests a coarser pilot dt or CSV, rather than writing a truncated file.

Step 4 - QC the sweep the machine actually made

Load measured sweep… opens a recorded pilot, ground-force or similarity trace and compares the trace you pick against the design. What to read:

  • Phase error vs time - the measured instantaneous phase minus the design. Industry practice holds the average within about 10° and the peaks within about 20-25°; the thresholds in the panel (avg phase ≤ °, peak phase ≤ °, THD ≤ %) are editable and saved with the preset, so set them to your contract's specification rather than to the defaults.
  • THD vs time - harmonic distortion. High distortion at low frequency usually means the vibrator hit its mass or flow limit, which is a machine limit, not a sweep design fault.
  • Envelope and spectrum overlays - drive drop-outs, decoupling and resonances show up here as differences between the measured and designed curves.
  • Correlation wavelet - designed against measured. A clean sweep reproduces the Klauder wavelet with its peak at zero lag.

The polarity trap, once per crew

Vibrator electronics following the SEG polarity standard have their accelerometers wired positive-up, while the SEG field-recording convention expects the first breaks of a compressive source to be negative. On Pelton-style systems the practical rule is: if the recorded similarity or ground-force polarity comes out inverted against the pilot, run the pilot at Phase = 180° rather than rewiring, and the correlation restores standard polarity. Check this once per crew and instrument combination with a similarity test before production, and do not assume it.

The advisories after a build - the force setpoint guidance and the low-frequency displacement limit - are guidance only. The vibrator's own limits always govern what the machine physically does, and the QC verdicts here flag numbers against your thresholds; whether to accept or re-shoot a vibrator point stays the observer's call.

8. Write and export the observer log for a production day

For: the observer.
Assumes: Part I. Nothing else - the Observer Log needs no seismic file open, though it can use a loaded SPS survey if you have one.

The job. One row per shot, all day, accurate enough that somebody can reconstruct the day from it a month later, and out at the end as a file you can send.

Step 1 - build the log once, at the start of the day

  1. Press O for the Observer Log.
  2. Work through the setup: the header information for the day, then which column groups you want, then the source type, then the rows. Choose the columns deliberately here - adding one later is easy, but a column you never fill in is noise on every row.
  3. Click Build log. The grid appears.

Do this once, not once per day. When the layout is how your crew wants it, use Save as… in the templates row to store the configured-but-empty log as a template. Every following day then starts from Load, and every day's log has the same columns - which is what makes a season's logs comparable. Export and Import move a template between machines as JSON.

Step 2 - fill it as you shoot

  • + Add row adds a shot; every cell is editable in place, and rows can be added, inserted, deleted and reordered.
  • PC clock stamps the time from the machine. NTP stamps the PC clock corrected by an offset measured against a time server: put the server in the box and click Sync to query it and compute the offset. Field laptops drift, and a log whose times are twenty seconds out is a log nobody can correlate with the recorder.
  • Import sources from SPS pulls the source stations from a loaded survey, so the station column is pre-filled in shooting order rather than typed.
  • Columns… opens the column manager to rename, reorder, retype, or add and remove columns after the fact.

Step 3 - when the shooting does not go to plan

A stuck shot, a re-shoot or a skipped point breaks the numbering for every row after it. There is no automatic detection and no hold button - deliberately, because guessing here is worse than not guessing. Instead, every shot-point and file-number cell is editable, and Renumber below… recomputes the shot point (and optionally the file number) for a chosen anchor row and every row after it. Set the Anchor row # and Anchor SP, give the Interval (± per row), and apply. That one dialog is how re-shoots, skips and drift get fixed.

Step 4 - let the recorder write the rows, if it can

Trigger Watch adds a highlighted row the moment a shot triggers, so the log keeps up with the crew instead of the other way round. Configure… picks the trigger system and its sources: for a Geometrics Geode setup, the SCS trigger event and the recorder's own file number; for other systems, any combination of watching the acquisition folder, tailing the SCS survey log, a UDP listener, or a serial trigger box on a COM port. Sound beeps on every trigger row.

Three things are worth knowing before you rely on it. A triggered row is pending and stays highlighted until you edit it - the log is yours and the watch only assists. When the shot file lands, the row is enriched with the file details rather than replaced. And the acquisition folder is only ever watched and read: SeisConv never writes into it.

Step 5 - save and export

  • Save log writes the whole log - header, columns and rows - as a JSON file, and Reload… opens it again. This is the working file: save it whenever you would save anything, and certainly before you close the app.
  • Export the day for the people who need to read it rather than edit it.
  • Clear log empties the rows and keeps the column configuration, which is what you want at the start of the next day if you are not using a template.

Every automatic write - a counter advance, an enrichment, a renumber - lands in the audit trail, signed with the name you set in Audit log and signature. That is what makes the log evidence rather than notes.

9. Two machines in the field with no network: move the files

For: any field pair - the recording laptop and the QC laptop.
Assumes: two Windows machines, both running SeisConv, and no router, no internet and no cloud account.

The job. The records are on the recording machine and you need them on the QC machine, all day, without walking a memory stick back and forth.

Step 1 - get the two machines onto the same WiFi

If there is a field WiFi, join both machines to it. If there is not, one machine can host one: fill in the hotspot Name and Password (at least eight characters), pick the WiFi adapter that will host it, and click Start hotspot. Status reads the live state, and the Host IP it reports is the address to give the other machine. Connect the second machine's WiFi to that hotspot the ordinary Windows way.

Step 2 - choose the folder and the role, on both machines

  • Choose… the folder that is kept identical. Everything under it is synced, recursively.
  • Role - Two-way (the default) has both machines push and pull. Master only serves and never pulls; Slave mirrors the master exactly, including deleting local extras. When one peer advertises Master or Slave the other adopts the complement automatically. For "records flow from the recorder to the QC laptop and nothing flows back", Master on the recorder and Slave on the QC machine says exactly that.
  • Sync mode - On change syncs the instant a file changes; Every N s polls on an interval. The rate limit caps transfer speed so a sync does not saturate a weak link.

Step 3 - start it

  1. Click Start WiFiSync on both machines. A peer should appear in the list within a few seconds.
  2. If it does not: click Open firewall ports… on both machines - it opens the two ports WiFiSync uses, for the Windows Private profile only. Then try Scan network, which actively sweeps the selected adapter's subnet instead of waiting for discovery broadcasts. As a last resort, type the peer's address into Peer IP and use Add peer.
  3. If the wrong adapter is selected - a machine usually has several - pick the WiFi or hotspot one in Adapter; on a hotspot host it is usually the 192.168.137.x one.
  4. Sync now forces a single pass immediately. The activity and transfer list shows what was pulled and deleted, newest first.

Two things to be careful about

  • The file server is unauthenticated by design. Anyone on the same network can talk to it. Run WiFiSync only on a field network you trust, or on the hotspot you host yourself.
  • Fix Hyper-V conflict appears only when the hotspot failed to start because a Hyper-V virtual switch is holding the WiFi adapter, and clicking it deletes that virtual switch after a UAC prompt. SeisConv cannot put it back, and any virtual machine, WSL or Docker network that was using the switch loses its connection until you recreate it yourself in Hyper-V Manager. If the machine runs virtual machines you care about, fix the conflict there instead and leave this button alone.

Deletions propagate, so a file deleted on one machine disappears on the other. There is a guard against the worst case - a peer whose drive has gone offline, and so reports an empty folder, cannot wipe your files - but "Slave" does mean "make this folder match the master, including removing what the master does not have".

10. A file will not open: diagnose it to a fix or a defensible verdict

For: everyone.
Assumes: Part I. This is the case that may end in "this file is corrupt, and here is the evidence", which is a perfectly good outcome as long as it is evidence and not a shrug.

The job. SeisConv refuses the file, or opens it and reports something that cannot be true. You need to end up either with a file that opens, or with a written verdict somebody else can act on.

First, the cheap checks

  1. Is the file complete? Compare its size on disk against another record from the same day. A file that is a fraction of its siblings was truncated in transfer or while being written - the commonest cause by a distance, and the one worth eliminating before you spend an hour on anything cleverer.
  2. Is it the file you think it is? Recorders write more than shot records: monitor files, test files, log files and index files often live in the same folder and often share an extension.
  3. Copy it again. If it came off a memory stick, over WiFiSync or from a network share, copy it once more and open the fresh copy. If the second copy opens, the first was damaged in transit and the source is fine.
  4. Try a sibling. Open a different record from the same folder. If every file in the folder fails, the problem is systematic - a recorder setting, a transfer that went wrong wholesale - and not this one file.

Read the message the app gave you

SeisConv is built not to guess. A malformed record is collected as an error and skipped rather than silently accepted, and a file whose header claims something impossible is refused rather than half-read into a plausible-looking lie. So the message is evidence, and it is worth copying verbatim into your report. Appendix C lists the error messages and what each one means.

The distinction to draw is between:

  • The file was refused outright - the format could not be identified, or the header is self-contradictory. Nothing was read.
  • The file opened, but with errors or a reduced trace count - the beginning was readable and something later was not. This usually means truncation, and it is often recoverable: the traces that did read are real traces.

When it opens but the numbers are wrong

A file that opens with an absurd trace count, an absurd sample count or a sample interval nobody would record at is telling you its header disagrees with its contents. Check what the Converter reports against what the crew actually shot, as in . Then look at the section (3): if the picture is coherent for part of the record and turns to hash partway down, the file was cut short; if it is hash from the first trace, the samples are not being read the way they were written.

Run the Health scan on what did open, as in . "Every trace dead from sample 400 onward" is a much more useful sentence in a report than "the file is bad".

Try converting the part that reads

If some of the record is readable, convert what opened to SEG-Y Rev 1 and check the output as in Part I. That gives you a clean file containing the traces that survived, which is often enough to keep the day's work moving while the original is investigated. Say clearly, in writing, that the output is a partial recovery - a rescued file that is not labelled as one will be treated as complete by whoever gets it next.

Writing the verdict

A defensible conclusion contains, in this order: what you were given (name, size, where it came from), what SeisConv reported (the exact message, or the exact counts it read), what you eliminated (re-copied, sibling files open normally, size against siblings), and what you concluded. For example: "The file is 4.1 MB where the other 27 records of the same configuration are 11.8 MB. SeisConv reads the first 38 of 96 traces and reports the remainder as errors. Two fresh copies from the source medium behave identically, and the neighbouring records open cleanly. Conclusion: this record was truncated at the recorder or during its first write, and cannot be recovered beyond trace 38. A partial SEG-Y of those 38 traces has been produced and is marked as a partial recovery."

That is a verdict somebody can act on. "It's corrupt" is not.
